title: How to Achieve “Success”
date: 2026-10-01

---

Success is subjective from person to person, but it’s something that almost everyone strives for in one way or another. Whether success means achieving a certain sports record, reaching a rank in a video game, building a billion-dollar company, or simply becoming a better version of yourself, I believe it all follows the same recipe.

## Ambition

Probably the catalyst of all intentional success, ambition is the north star.

You have to want something before you can ever begin working toward it. Ambition gives you a reason to sacrifice comfort, take risks, and spend time doing things that other people might not understand.

I also think there is a difference between wanting something and being ambitious. Almost everyone wants more money, a better career, a better body, or a better life. Ambition is when that desire becomes strong enough to influence your decisions.

It gives you direction. Without ambition, persistence and consistency have nowhere to go.

## Delusion

I actually think a certain level of delusion is necessary for success.

Almost every ambitious goal sounds unreasonable at the beginning. If you only pursue things that you already know you can accomplish, you will probably never find out what you are actually capable of.

Sometimes, you almost have to lie to yourself about who you are until you start becoming that person. You have to tell yourself that you are confident before you fully feel confident, that you are disciplined before discipline comes naturally, or that you are capable of achieving something before you have any real proof.

The thoughts you constantly feed yourself shape the way you see yourself, and the way you see yourself influences how you act. If you repeatedly tell yourself that you are lazy, incapable, awkward, or destined to fail, eventually you start behaving in ways that reinforce those beliefs.

The opposite can also be true. When you repeatedly identify with the person you want to become, your actions slowly begin to match that identity.

At first, it may feel fake. Eventually, it becomes familiar.

That does not mean completely ignoring reality or pretending your weaknesses do not exist. The useful kind of delusion is believing that who you are today does not have to be who you are tomorrow. Sometimes you have to create the identity first and grow into it afterward.

## Persistence

Ambition gets you started. Persistence keeps you going when ambition stops feeling exciting.

Almost anything worth accomplishing eventually becomes difficult. Plans fail. Opportunities disappear. People reject you. Progress slows down. At some point, motivation is no longer enough.

This is where a lot of people quit.

Persistence is the willingness to keep moving despite having evidence that things are not going exactly as planned. It does not mean repeating the same failed strategy forever. Sometimes persistence means changing your approach while refusing to abandon the larger goal.

Failure becomes much less intimidating when you start viewing it as information rather than a final result.

## Consistency

Persistence and consistency sound similar, but I think there is an important difference.

Persistence is continuing after something goes wrong. Consistency is continuing when nothing particularly interesting is happening.

Most progress is boring.

Going to the gym once is easy. Going four times a week for years is difficult. Working intensely on a business idea for three days is easy. Working on it when there is no attention, money, or immediate payoff is much harder.

The small actions rarely feel significant in the moment, but they compound.

You do not need to have an incredible day every day. You just need enough ordinary days moving in the right direction.

## Kindness

Kindness might seem out of place compared to the other traits, but I think it becomes increasingly important the further you go.

Very few meaningful accomplishments are completely individual. At some point, success involves other people. Coworkers, mentors, employees, friends, customers, partners, or even strangers can influence where you end up.

Being someone people genuinely enjoy working with matters.

You can be ambitious without treating every relationship like a transaction. You can be competitive without hoping other people fail. You can become successful without becoming someone you would have disliked before you were successful.

In the long run, your reputation tends to travel ahead of you.

## The Recipe

None of these traits work particularly well alone.

- Ambition without consistency becomes daydreaming.
- Delusion without self-awareness becomes arrogance.
- Persistence without adaptation becomes stubbornness.
- Consistency without direction becomes wasted effort.
- Success without kindness can leave you with achievements that nobody wants to celebrate with you.

I do not think there is one guaranteed formula for success. Luck, timing, circumstances, and opportunity obviously matter.

But if I had to bet on someone succeeding over a long enough period of time, I would bet on the person who is ambitious enough to aim high, delusional enough to believe it can happen, persistent enough to survive failure, consistent enough to keep showing up, and kind enough to bring other people along with them.

## Reflection

When I think about success in my own life, I sometimes wonder whether I have already achieved it or if I am still chasing it.

On paper, I have accomplished a lot of things that I am proud of. I have built the body I wanted, I am building a career for myself, and I am on track to graduate with an MBA at 22 years old. I also have something that is probably more valuable than any accomplishment on a resume: a healthy family.

At the same time, I do not feel like I have “made it.” I think that is because my definition of success has always included both material and intrinsic goals.

I do want the material things. I want a nice house, a nice car, and the freedom to live comfortably without constantly thinking about money. I want to build a strong career, make good money, and reach a point where finances create opportunities instead of limitations. I do not necessarily see those goals as shallow. To me, money is valuable because of what it allows me to provide and experience.

But the deeper version of success for me is much more personal.

One day, I want to build a family where my wife and kids never have to worry about whether we can afford something important. I want to be able to provide for them without having to look at the price tag first. Whether that means education, travel, healthcare, hobbies, or simply experiences together, I want to create a life where money does not become a constant source of stress.

Even then, I do not think providing financially would be enough.

I want to have a healthy relationship with my wife. I want my kids to feel comfortable talking to me and to know that I am present in their lives, not just someone who pays for everything. I want to maintain meaningful friendships and relationships with the people around me. There would be little value in building an impressive career or making a lot of money if I sacrificed every relationship that actually made life worth living.

That is what makes success difficult to define. By some standards, I could probably say that I am already successful. I have achieved goals that a younger version of myself would have been extremely proud of. But I also know that many of the things I care about most cannot be accomplished yet.

Maybe success is not a point you eventually reach. Maybe it is being able to look at your life at different stages and recognize that you are becoming the person you once wanted to be, while still having something meaningful to work toward.

Right now, I think that is where I am.
